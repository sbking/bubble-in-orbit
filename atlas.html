The floor & liftoff

Your Tectonic Sub runs 36 to 69 Hz while MEGA Bass sits at F♯1–E2 (46–82 Hz). Down here notes sit brutally close in hertz: D1 and E1 are only 4.5 Hz apart.

An FFT splits audio into equal-width bins (sampleRate / fftSize). At 2048 a bin is over 20 Hz wide so D1 and E1 blur together; at 32768 bins shrink under 1.5 Hz, but the 0.7 s analysis window smears fast bass attacks in time.

Jump straight to Bar 11 Hit #1, Bar 25 Liftoff, or Bar 31 Hit #2, and flip Fold to mono to see how cleanly the low end holds center.

When we first tried building the 3.5-bar MEGA Bass riser into the fugue (Bars 25–28), it sounded mysteriously flat. Why? Because the bass chain ends with a Compressor + Limiter at −16 dB.

Ramping gain before a limiter just makes the limiter work harder and squash the rise back flat. Moving the resonant lowpass sweep (155 Hz → 4.1 kHz, Q=1.95) and gain curve post-limiter — then dropping cleanly to silence on Bar 28 Beats 3–4 — unlocked the real underwater-to-surface launch.

Sixty-four kilobytes

“Trying to squeeze as much sonic content from the 64k memory limitation of the SNES, I was very much inspired by the Korg Wavestation. It didn't have resonant filters, but used short wave samples, many of them single cycle waveforms, and Korg used a technique they called "Wave Sequencing" to put them back together in a harmonically pleasing order.” David Wise, interviewed by Chris Greening, Square Enix Music Online

The SNES S-DSP has 64 KiB of RAM shared by its driver, samples, and echo buffer. Samples compress as BRR (16 samples into 9 bytes) at 32 kHz.

Flip the budget switch to run any of your crystal stems through real mono 32 kHz BRR encoding + the chip's 16 ms-stepped echo (capped at 240 ms — shorter than one of your 96 BPM eighth notes).

A 32-sample single-cycle waveform costs only 18 bytes in BRR. Chaining single cycles with per-step lengths and crossfades creates a wave sequence — an evolving Wavestation timbre from a sliver of RAM.

Drag a step's right edge for length, the diamond for crossfade, and draw directly onto the waveform to sculpt the 16 harmonics live over your pad chords.

Joystick & HalfTime

Built by Chris Meyer at Sequential Circuits in 1985 and carried into Korg's Wavestation: four sources and one XY point whose bilinear weights always sum to 1, shifting timbre without changing loudness.

Switch between Ambient Orbit (Pads / Halo / Shimmer / Rhodes) and Crystal Cathedral (Pads / Lead V1 / Halo V2 / Fugue V3 on Bars 29–36) — drag the bubble or record a bar-synced looping path.

How does Cableguys-style HalfTime FX slow a stem to half speed and drop it exactly one octave down while staying locked to the song's tempo?

A circular ring buffer with two pointers: the gold Write Head records live audio at 1.0× speed, while the purple Read Head trails behind at 0.5× speed. Every Beat 1 (or half-bar), the read head snaps back to the write head with a 6 ms raised-cosine crossfade — preserving Beat-1 drum punch with zero drift.

Your ears are doing some of this

Two tones a few hertz apart sum into one tone whose loudness swells at their difference frequency: 220 Hz against 222 Hz beats twice a second.

Spread more voices across cents and the wobble turns into luminous shimmer — the exact mechanism inside Surge XT's unison voices and chorus across your crystal lead and pads.

Equal temperament spaces every major third at 400 cents; the pure 5:4 harmonic ratio is 386.3 cents. In your Aadd9 voicing (A3 + C♯4), A3's 5th harmonic (1100 Hz) beats nearly 9 times a second against an equal-tempered C♯4's 4th harmonic (1108.7 Hz).

Watch two spectrum peaks merge into one when you flip to the pure third.

Play harmonics 2, 3, 4, and 5 of a bass note with zero energy at the fundamental — your auditory cortex still perceives the missing pitch.

That's why your saturated MEGA Bass survives laptop speakers (its tape/phaser harmonics reconstruct the root) while the pure-sine Tectonic Sub disappears under a 150 Hz high-pass cut.
